## Critical production rules

1. Use the narration below verbatim unless a medical reviewer approves an edit. Do not let text-to-speech read headings, stage directions, labels, or bracketed instructions.
2. Generate/record narration first, then time visuals to the actual spoken audio. Do not speed up speech unnaturally to force it into a scene.
3. Voice: natural, calm, authoritative adult English narrator; warm documentary delivery; neutral international accent; steady pace; crisp consonants; no whispering, dramatic rasp, or excessive pauses.
4. Narration must be intelligible above music. Mix narration around -16 LUFS integrated for the finished stereo program, with true peak no higher than -1 dBTP. Keep background music approximately 18–24 dB below the voice and duck it further under speech. Avoid clipping, distortion, aggressive noise reduction, or hard cuts in words.
5. Use real provider output only. Never treat simulated narration or placeholder visuals as a finished video.
6. Every scene must match the narration currently being spoken. Use cutaways only at the corresponding phrase. Add brief visual transitions (about 6–12 frames) where suitable, but never cover speech with a loud effect.
7. Use readable mobile-safe on-screen text, high contrast, short labels, and generous margins. Proofread all generated labels; replace AI-garbled text with editor-rendered typography.
8. Medical content is educational, not diagnostic. Avoid implying that any one symptom proves kidney disease. Use medically reviewed, licensed assets and cite reliable sources in the video description before publication.
9. Keep the output private/unlisted during quality assurance. Do not publish automatically until a human checks the rendered video, voice clarity, facts, rights, and disclosure requirements.

### Scene 2 — Persistent puffy eyes | 0:25–0:55
**Visuals:** Person notices morning puffiness in a mirror; transition to a detailed kidney filtration barrier. Show albumin crossing a damaged barrier into the forming urine. Keep the molecular animation clear and anatomically grounded.
**Voice-over:**
> Sign number one: Persistent puffiness around your eyes, especially in the morning.
>
> Occasional puffiness can result from poor sleep, allergies, or other common causes. But persistent swelling, particularly around your eyes or in your feet and ankles, deserves attention.
>
> When the kidneys' filtering barriers become damaged, they can allow albumin—a protein normally retained in your blood—to leak into your urine.
>
> When blood protein levels fall, fluid can accumulate in body tissues, sometimes causing noticeable puffiness.
>
> Puffy eyes alone don't prove you have kidney disease. But persistent, unexplained swelling is worth discussing with a healthcare professional.
**On-screen text:** SIGN 1: PERSISTENT PUFFY EYES; molecular label: ALBUMIN (BLOOD PROTEIN) LEAKING
**Audio:** Gentle transition; no distracting effects under narration.

### Scene 3 — Persistent foamy urine | 0:55–1:35
**Visuals:** Split screen with two comparable, non-graphic bathroom illustrations. Left: ordinary bubbles dissipate naturally. Right: denser foam remains during an illustrative timer. Do not imply a specific number of seconds diagnoses disease. Transition to glomerular filtration barrier and albumin molecules.
**Voice-over:**
> Sign number two: Foamy urine that keeps happening.
>
> A few bubbles are usually nothing to worry about. A fast stream or concentrated urine can create them naturally.
>
> But persistent, dense foam can sometimes indicate protein leaking into your urine.
>
> Think of healthy kidneys like an ultra-fine kitchen strainer. They allow waste products to pass through while retaining essential proteins, including albumin.
>
> When this microscopic filtration barrier becomes damaged, protein can slip through into your urine.
>
> This is called albuminuria, and it can be an early sign of kidney disease—even when you feel perfectly normal.
>
> Remember, foam alone cannot tell you whether protein is present. A urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio test, known as uACR, can check for abnormal albumin leakage.
**On-screen text:** SIGN 2: PERSISTENT FOAMY URINE; labels ORDINARY BUBBLES and PERSISTENT FOAM; disclaimer: Illustration only—not a diagnostic test.
**Editing:** If a timer is used, label it illustrative only. Never present “foam remains 30 seconds” as a diagnostic threshold. Keep the disclaimer readable on mobile.

### Scene 4 — Persistent, unexplained itching | 1:35–2:10
**Visuals:** Person gently scratches forearm; cut to transparent body with kidneys and bloodstream; restrained graphics indicate mineral-balance changes and nerve signaling. Avoid showing specific toxins as confirmed causes in a generic patient.
**Voice-over:**
> Sign number three: Persistent itching without an obvious cause.
>
> Dry skin, allergies, and common skin conditions are frequent causes of itching.
>
> But advanced kidney disease can also cause persistent itching, sometimes across large areas of the body.
>
> When kidney function declines significantly, the body's balance of minerals and other substances can change. Inflammation and changes in how nerves process sensations may also contribute.
>
> The result can be uncomfortable itching without an obvious explanation.
>
> Itching alone is not a reliable way to identify kidney disease. But if it persists, particularly alongside swelling, unusual fatigue, or other concerning symptoms, speak with a healthcare professional.
>
> And there's another important clue to understand: what happens when your kidneys struggle to clear waste from your bloodstream?
**On-screen text:** SIGN 3: PERSISTENT, UNEXPLAINED ITCHING
**Transition:** Camera travels through blood vessels toward the mouth/salivary glands as the narration mentions waste.

### Scene 5 — Metallic taste or unusual breath | 2:10–2:45
**Visuals:** Person reacts subtly to an unusual taste; anatomically plausible mouth and salivary glands; bloodstream and kidneys; restrained molecule overlay. Do not show graphic mouth imagery.
**Voice-over:**
> When the kidneys struggle to clear waste, it doesn't just affect processes throughout the body. In advanced disease, waste products can also affect your mouth and breath.
>
> Sign number four: A persistent metallic taste or ammonia-like breath.
>
> Dental problems, medications, infections, and acid reflux can all cause these changes.
>
> But when kidney function becomes severely reduced, urea can accumulate in the blood. Urea entering saliva can be converted into ammonia, contributing to an unpleasant taste or ammonia-like breath.
>
> A strange taste after one meal doesn't mean kidney disease. But persistent, unexplained changes deserve medical attention, especially when other symptoms are present.
>
> Sometimes, the warning isn't pain. It's a change you barely notice.
**On-screen text:** SIGN 4: PERSISTENT METALLIC TASTE OR UNUSUAL BREATH
**Audio:** Subtle transition; no unpleasant sound effects.

### Scene 6 — Unexplained fatigue | 2:45–3:25
**Visuals:** Tired person at desk; 3D kidney emits an EPO signal; follow signal to bone marrow; red blood cells enter circulation and carry oxygen. Keep the EPO-to-red-cell sequence scientifically clear.
**Voice-over:**
> And when kidney function declines, oxygen delivery can become another problem.
>
> Sign number five: Fatigue that doesn't seem to go away.
>
> If you constantly feel exhausted, even after sleeping, don't automatically blame your busy schedule.
>
> Healthy kidneys produce a hormone called erythropoietin, or EPO. It signals your bone marrow to produce red blood cells, which carry oxygen throughout your body.
>
> As kidney disease advances, the kidneys may produce less EPO. This can contribute to anemia, leaving you feeling weak, tired, dizzy, or unable to concentrate.
>
> Some people with kidney disease also experience restless legs, although this symptom has many possible causes.
>
> Fatigue doesn't automatically mean kidney damage. But persistent exhaustion deserves investigation, particularly if you have diabetes, high blood pressure, or other kidney disease risk factors.
**On-screen text:** SIGN 5: UNEXPLAINED FATIGUE
**Flow diagram, reveal one element at a time:** KIDNEYS → EPO SIGNAL → BONE MARROW → RED BLOOD CELLS → OXYGEN DELIVERY
